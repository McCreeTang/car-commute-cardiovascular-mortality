# Prolonged car commuting and cardiovascular risk in the Bay Area: literature review

Long hours in a car are associated with obesity, lower physical activity and higher blood pressure in most studies that measured car time directly, but the evidence that car time itself raises cardiovascular event rates rests on a single cohort of US men and is contradicted by two null cohorts. Total daily sitting has a consistent, modest, non-linear association with cardiovascular mortality (about 2% per additional hour, steeper beyond 6 to 8 hours a day) that largely disappears in highly active people. Anger roughly doubles the short-term odds of myocardial infarction and raises the odds of stroke, yet no retrieved study measured anger while driving against a cardiovascular endpoint. The planning literature documents rising commute distances in high-housing-cost California regions, and no retrieved study connects that trend to cardiovascular outcomes. The Bay Area study therefore has to borrow most of its dose-response functions from generic sitting and generic anger research, and it must declare that transfer as an assumption.

## What the evidence establishes

### Car time and commuting distance

Time in cars tracks adiposity more reliably than it tracks any other cardiometabolic marker. In 37,570 Australian drivers aged 47 to 75, the odds of obesity rose stepwise with daily driving time, reaching an odds ratio of 1.78 (95% CI 1.61 to 1.97) for more than two hours a day against 30 minutes or less, with parallel gradients for insufficient physical activity and short sleep ([Ding 2014](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0094602)). Australian adults who sat in cars for more than an hour a day had a body-mass index 0.77 kg/m2 higher (95% CI 0.16 to 1.38) than those reporting 15 minutes or less, after adjustment for leisure activity, diet, television and occupational sitting ([Sugiyama 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ypmed.2015.11.029)), and an Atlanta travel survey of 10,878 people estimated 6% higher odds of obesity per additional daily hour in a car ([Frank 2004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amepre.2004.04.011)). Two systematic reviews reach the same conclusion: eight of ten studies in the earlier review found a positive association between motor-vehicle travel and weight status ([McCormack 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ypmed.2014.06.002)), and the update that restricted exposure to car use found detrimental associations with obesity outcomes in six of six duration analyses, with mixed or null findings for glucose, lipids and blood pressure and no study using device-measured car time ([Sugiyama 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jth.2020.100930)). Daily car commuters in Adelaide gained 2.18 kg over four years against 1.26 kg for non-car commuters, a difference whose trend test did not reach significance (P = 0.090) ([Sugiyama 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amepre.2012.09.063)), and a survey of 158 residents on the fringe of Sydney found twice the odds of obesity with commutes longer than 45 minutes (OR 2.04, 95% CI 1.05 to 3.96) ([Parise 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph19010410)).

Commuting distance adds a blood-pressure signal that the car-time studies do not show. Among 4,297 employed adults examined at a Texas preventive-medicine clinic, a one-way commute longer than 20 miles carried odds ratios of 1.35 (95% CI 1.10 to 1.65) for elevated blood pressure and 0.66 (0.54 to 0.82) for meeting physical-activity recommendations relative to 0 to 5 miles, and the obesity association weakened to 1.10 (0.84 to 1.44) once fitness and activity entered the model ([Hoehner 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amepre.2012.02.020)). That attenuation suggests displaced physical activity is the main pathway for adiposity, while the blood-pressure association persisted. Systolic pressure, by contrast, was not significantly higher with long car time in the Australian data (1.60 mmHg, 95% CI -0.18 to 3.37) ([Sugiyama 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ypmed.2015.11.029)), transport sitting was unrelated to a clustered cardiometabolic risk score in a second Australian analysis ([Dempsey 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12966-018-0748-3)), and in 208,893 UK Biobank commuters each additional 10 miles of weekly commuting changed the odds of an out-of-range lipid by less than 1% ([Kaiser 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ypmed.2023.107521)).

Longitudinal and time-use studies point to behaviour displacement as the mechanism. US workers trade roughly 16% of each extra commuting hour against physical activity and 28 to 35% against sleep ([Christian 2012](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11524-012-9678-6)), and each additional commuting hour was associated with 15 minutes less actigraphy-measured sleep among Hispanic and Latino workers ([Petrov 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amepre.2017.11.006)). Within-person analyses of Swedish workers found higher odds of physical inactivity when commuting more than five hours a week, but only among those also working more than 40 hours (OR 1.25, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.51) ([Halonen 2020](https://doi.org/10.1136/oemed-2019-106173)), and a 6% increase in the odds of inactivity per doubling of commuting distance ([Raza 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ypmed.2021.106665)). British panel data showed lower subjective health with longer commutes, most clearly among car drivers and women, while objective health measures barely moved ([Künn-Nelen 2015](https://doi.org/10.1002/hec.3199)), and a Swedish survey found a flat or concave relation between car-commute duration and health complaints ([Hansson 2011](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2458-11-834)).

Hard cardiovascular endpoints for car time are scarce and inconsistent. Men who rode in a car more than 10 hours a week had a multivariable hazard ratio for cardiovascular death of 1.50 (95% CI 1.08 to 2.09) against fewer than 4 hours over 21 years of follow-up ([Warren 2010](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e3181c3aa7e)); this is the only driving-specific cardiovascular mortality estimate found by the sweep, and the car-use review likewise identified a single such study ([Sugiyama 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jth.2020.100930)). Spanish university graduates showed no significant all-cause mortality gradient with driving time (incidence rate ratio 1.14 per two hours a day, 95% CI 0.90 to 1.44) ([Basterra-Gortari 2014](https://doi.org/10.1161/jaha.114.000864)), and sitting in a car or bus was unrelated to mortality in 134,596 participants of the Multiethnic Cohort ([Kim 2013](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyt108)). The comparative literature on commute mode is larger: in UK Biobank, regular commuters with more active travel patterns had a hazard ratio for fatal cardiovascular disease of 0.70 (95% CI 0.51 to 0.95) against exclusive car users ([Panter 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/heartjnl-2017-312699)), cycle commuters had roughly half the incident cardiovascular disease of non-active commuters ([Celis-Morales 2017](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.j1456)), and rail commuters in England and Wales had 21% lower cardiovascular mortality than private-vehicle commuters ([Patterson 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/s2542-5196%2820%2930079-6)). Pooled estimates are smaller, with a 9% lower cardiovascular incidence for active commuting and no association with cardiovascular mortality ([Dinu 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-1023-0)), lower pooled risks of obesity (0.88) and diabetes (0.82) but not hypertension (0.95, 95% CI 0.87 to 1.04) ([Wu 2021](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjgh-2021-005838)), and a New Zealand census cohort found no cause-specific mortality differences by mode ([Shaw 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyz257)). These comparisons show that car commuting is the least favourable mode; they do not show that more hours of it are worse.

Occupational drivers supply supporting physiology. Long-haul truck drivers younger than 55 had excess proportionate ischaemic heart disease mortality in US death records ([Robinson 2005](https://doi.org/10.1002/ajim.20126)), long driving hours in Taipei taxi drivers were associated with higher white-cell and platelet counts ([Chen 2005](https://doi.org/10.1136/oem.2005.020354)), job overcommitment predicted cardiovascular events in Taiwanese bus drivers ([Wu 2019](https://doi.org/10.1097/ede.0000000000001003)), and Tokyo office workers commuting 90 minutes or more each way showed a sympathetic-dominant heart-rate variability profile ([Kageyama 1998](https://doi.org/10.2486/indhealth.36.209)).

### Sitting time and physical activity

The association between total sitting and cardiovascular disease is consistent in direction, modest in size and concentrated at high exposures. The dose-response meta-analysis that adjusted for physical activity estimated a relative risk for cardiovascular mortality of 1.02 per additional hour of daily sitting (95% CI 1.01 to 1.03; five cohorts), with almost no gradient below 6 hours (1.01, 0.99 to 1.02) and 1.04 (1.03 to 1.04) per hour above it ([Patterson 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10654-018-0380-1)). Category-based syntheses agree on magnitude: hazard or risk ratios for the highest against the lowest sitting category of 1.14 for incident cardiovascular disease ([Pandey 2016](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamacardio.2016.1567)), 1.18 for cardiovascular mortality ([Biswas 2015](https://doi.org/10.7326/m14-1651)), 1.29 for fatal and non-fatal events ([Onagbiye 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ypmed.2023.107812)) and 1.29 for cardiovascular mortality ([Jingjie 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-022-12728-6)). The PURE cohort across 21 countries reported 1.21 (95% CI 1.10 to 1.34) for major cardiovascular disease at 8 or more hours against fewer than 4 ([Li 2022](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamacardio.2022.1581)), and a Danish cohort reported 1.38 (1.01 to 1.88) for myocardial infarction at 10 or more hours ([Petersen 2014](https://doi.org/10.1186/1479-5868-11-13)). Device-based studies give larger estimates, 1.62 (1.34 to 1.96) for cardiovascular death above 10.6 hours a day against 8.2 to 9.4 hours in UK Biobank ([Ajufo 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2024.10.065)) and a pooled 1.89 (1.09 to 3.29) across five accelerometer cohorts ([Liew 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.focus.2022.100054)), and accelerometer-based gradients for all-cause mortality are steeper still, reaching 2.63 (1.94 to 3.56) in the most sedentary quarter ([Ekelund 2019b](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.l4570)). Among 92,234 postmenopausal women the highest sitting category carried hazard ratios of 1.13 (0.99 to 1.29) for cardiovascular and 1.27 (1.04 to 1.55) for coronary mortality ([Seguin 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amepre.2013.10.021)).

Physical activity modifies the association strongly. In harmonised data on more than a million adults, sitting more than 8 hours a day carried no excess mortality in the most active quartile (HR 1.04, 95% CI 0.99 to 1.10) and a 59% excess in the least active ([Ekelund 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0140-6736%2816%2930370-1)); the cardiovascular-mortality analysis found 9 to 32% higher risk across sitting categories only in the least active quartile ([Ekelund 2019a](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-098963)), and the same pattern appeared in the Australian 45 and Up cohort ([Stamatakis 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2019.02.031)). Adjustment for activity roughly halves the pooled association with incident disease, from 1.29 to 1.14 ([Bailey 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amepre.2019.04.015)), and a 2026 synthesis of joint exposures estimates 1.78 for low activity with high sitting against 1.16 for high activity with high sitting ([Lunar 2026](https://doi.org/10.1093/eurjpc/zwag263)). Sitting has also been rising in the United States, from 5.5 to 6.4 hours a day among adults between 2007 and 2016 ([Yang 2019](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2019.3636)).

The domain of sitting matters, which is the central obstacle to transferring these functions to commuting. Leisure and television sitting carry the steepest gradients ([Patterson 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10654-018-0380-1)), occupational sitting gave a hazard ratio of 1.34 for cardiovascular death in a Taiwanese cohort of 481,688 ([Gao 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2023.50680)) but was judged inconclusive in pooled analysis ([Jingjie 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-022-12728-6)), and transport sitting, where it has been separated, has mostly been null for mortality ([Kim 2013](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyt108); [Basterra-Gortari 2014](https://doi.org/10.1161/jaha.114.000864)). Australian drivers with more than two hours of daily driving were also slightly less likely to report 8 or more hours of total sitting (OR 0.88) ([Ding 2014](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0094602)), so car time cannot be assumed to add one-for-one to total sitting.

### Anger, driving stress and traffic

Episodes of anger are an established short-term trigger of acute cardiovascular events. Case-crossover estimates for myocardial infarction in the one to two hours after an outburst cluster between 2.3 and 2.4: 2.3 (95% CI 1.7 to 3.2) in the first Onset Study report ([Mittleman 1995](https://doi.org/10.1161/01.cir.92.7.1720)), 2.43 (2.01 to 2.90) in the full cohort of 3,886 patients with a gradient by anger intensity ([Mostofsky 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amjcard.2013.03.035)), and 2.44 (99% CI 2.06 to 2.89) across 12,461 cases in 52 countries, where 14.4% of cases reported anger or emotional upset in the hour before onset ([Smyth 2016](https://doi.org/10.1161/circulationaha.116.023142)). The corresponding odds ratio for stroke was 1.37 (99% CI 1.15 to 1.64), and 2.05 for intracerebral haemorrhage ([Smyth 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehab738)). A meta-analysis of nine case-crossover studies confirmed the direction in every study with very high heterogeneity (I2 of 92.5% for coronary events) ([Mostofsky 2014](https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehu033)).

Chronic anger and stress carry smaller relative risks than acute episodes. Prospective studies pool to a hazard ratio of 1.19 (95% CI 1.05 to 1.35) for coronary disease with trait anger or hostility, with signs of publication bias ([Chida 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2008.11.044)); frequent strong anger was associated with cardiovascular mortality (1.23, 1.09 to 1.40) and heart failure but not myocardial infarction in 47,077 Swedish adults ([Titova 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/ehjopen/oeac050)); the pooled association with stroke was null (1.08, 0.79 to 1.47) ([Chen 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00415-019-09231-1)); and high perceived stress gave 1.27 (1.12 to 1.45) for coronary disease ([Richardson 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amjcard.2012.08.004)). Larger blood-pressure responses to laboratory stress predict worse later cardiovascular status ([Chida 2010](https://doi.org/10.1161/hypertensionaha.109.146621)).

Driving in traffic produces measurable physiological responses, but their link to disease is inferred. Being in traffic preceded myocardial infarction onset by an hour with an odds ratio of 2.92 (95% CI 2.22 to 3.83) in the Augsburg registry, with car use the commonest exposure ([Peters 2004](https://doi.org/10.1056/nejmoa040203)); the design cannot separate emotional stress from exhaust or noise. A systematic review found moderate evidence that long hours of on-road driving raise urinary catecholamines and cortisol and insufficient evidence for shorter drives ([Antoun 2017](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0185517)); heart rate, R-R interval and pupil diameter are the measures most sensitive to driver stress across experiments ([Kerautret 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnrgo.2021.756473)); peak traffic raised catecholamines in urban bus drivers ([Evans 1991](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.76.5.658)); and Lebanese drivers measured after congestion had systolic pressures of 142 against 123 mmHg ([Bou Samra 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/jch.13102)). The pollution pathway has trial support: unfiltered cabin air during a two-hour drive raised diastolic pressure by 4.7 mmHg (95% CI 0.9 to 8.4) relative to filtered air in a small randomised crossover study ([Young 2023](https://doi.org/10.7326/m23-1309)), and a scripted two-hour highway commute lowered heart-rate variability ([Sarnat 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envres.2014.05.004)). Residential road-traffic noise adds about 2 to 3% to cardiovascular incidence per 10 dB ([Pershagen 2025](https://doi.org/10.1097/ee9.0000000000000400); [Fu 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11524-023-00769-0)).

Road rage has been measured as a behaviour and never as a cardiovascular exposure. Roughly a third of Ontario adults admitted shouting or gesturing at another driver in the previous year and almost half had been on the receiving end ([Smart 2003](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf03403543)), victimisation fell from 47.5% to 40.6% between 2001 and 2003 ([Smart 2005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsr.2005.03.005)), and angry or threatening driving was related to crash involvement in a US sample ([Wells-Parker 2002](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0001-4575%2801%2900021-5)). The psychological literature on congestion, driver stress and aggression is long-standing ([Hennessy 1997](https://doi.org/10.1080/001401397188198); [Hennessy 1999](https://doi.org/10.1002/%28sici%291098-2337%281999%2925%3A6%3C409%3A%3Aaid-ab2%3E3.0.co%3B2-0); [Novaco 1990](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf00931303)). On title screening, none of the 32 PubMed records combining road rage, aggressive driving or driving anger with cardiovascular terms reported a cardiovascular event.

### Housing costs and Bay Area commutes

Housing scarcity near jobs lengthens commutes, and the Bay Area is a documented case. Between 2002 and 2015 Bay Area employment grew 17% while mean commute distance rose from 17.2 to 17.8 miles, and from 19.1 to 20.8 miles for workers in outlying job centres ([Blumenberg 2024a](https://doi.org/10.5198/jtlu.2024.2456)). In Los Angeles, a higher ratio of jobs to affordable housing units predicts longer commutes ([Blumenberg 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11116-022-10264-1)); national travel-survey data show commute distances rising for low-income and higher-income workers alike, with the increase among low-income workers attributed largely to a shift toward low-density neighbourhoods ([Blumenberg 2019](https://doi.org/10.2148/benv.45.4.563)); Irish gravity models attribute up to 0.6 minutes of one-way commute to each 10% rise in employment-centre rents ([Ahrens 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/0042098020910698)); and US wage data imply substantial compensating differentials for long commutes and high housing costs ([Mayock 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/1540-6229.12097)). One-way commutes of 90 minutes or more behave as a constrained choice ([Marion 2007](https://doi.org/10.3141/2013-06)). Earlier Bay Area work described its polycentric form, the high drive-alone share of suburban job centres and the inequality in job accessibility and commuting time between cars and public transit ([Cervero 1997](https://doi.org/10.1068/a290865); [Kawabata 2007](https://doi.org/10.1080/00420980701426616)), and related studies cover jobs-housing balance and fit, long-distance commuting in California and residential instability in the region ([Cervero 1996](https://doi.org/10.1080/01944369608975714); [Benner 2016](https://doi.org/10.1080/02723638.2015.1112565); [Mitra 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtrangeo.2019.102489); [Blumenberg 2024b](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cities.2024.104842); [Hwang 2021](https://doi.org/10.24148/cdrb2021-02)).

The health side of this literature stops short of commuting. Gentrification classifications for the nine counties disagree sharply, flagging 5.2%, 6.1% or 46.7% of 1,580 tracts depending on the method ([Mujahid 2019](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph16122246)). Housing instability has mixed but generally adverse associations with hypertension, diabetes and cardiovascular disease ([Gu 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-023-15875-6)), zip-code housing cost burden predicts cardiovascular hospitalisation among Medicaid beneficiaries with heart failure (OR 1.03 per 10 percentage points, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.06) ([Ceasar 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamahealthforum.2025.5903)), and high rents under gentrification contribute to food insecurity among chronically ill Bay Area residents living on disability income ([Whittle 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2015.08.027)); a California study relates gentrification to self-rated health ([Izenberg 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.healthplace.2018.06.002)). Sprawl is associated with obesity and hypertension at county level ([Ewing 2003](https://doi.org/10.4278/0890-1171-18.1.47)) and with coronary events in women ([Griffin 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.healthplace.2012.11.003)). The closest precedent for the proposed design is a county-level California analysis of 2001 survey data in which obesity ranked with vehicle miles travelled and with commute time ([Lopez-Zetina 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.healthplace.2005.09.001)); a city-level test in 178 Latin American cities found no association between peak-hour car travel time and obesity or diabetes ([Delclòs-Alió 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cities.2022.103899)). In-vehicle particulate exposure has been measured on Bay Area commutes without health outcomes ([Deevi 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/atmos15091130)).

## Differences by age

Age-specific estimates are sparse, and where they exist the relative risks are similar or larger in younger adults. For car time, the cardiovascular-mortality trend was significant among men younger than 60 (P for trend 0.0009) with a borderline age interaction (P = 0.07), and the stratum hazard ratios were published only as a figure ([Warren 2010](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e3181c3aa7e)); commuting-distance associations showed no interaction with age ([Hoehner 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amepre.2012.02.020)); car use and obesity were more strongly related in working-age than in older adults ([Sugiyama 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jth.2020.100930)); and prolonged sitting in cars is most prevalent in middle age ([Sugiyama 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ypmed.2012.07.026)). For sitting, the hazard ratio for more than 6 hours of sedentary leisure a day was 1.72 (95% CI 1.29 to 2.30) below age 60 and 1.19 (1.05 to 1.36) at 60 and above (P for interaction < 0.001) ([Larsson 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2018.11.020)), whereas a between-study contrast found 1.27 (1.20 to 1.35) in cohorts younger than a median 58.6 years and 1.37 (1.16 to 1.62) in older cohorts with no significant difference ([Onagbiye 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ypmed.2023.107812)). Other cohorts report consistency across age groups ([van der Ploeg 2012](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinternmed.2011.2174); [Katzmarzyk 2009](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e3181930355)) or a stronger association in women aged 70 and over ([Chomistek 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2013.03.031)).

For anger, exposure is more common in the young while relative risk varies little. Anger or emotional upset preceded stroke in 11.5% of cases younger than 45, 10.3% of those aged 45 to 65 and 7.2% of those older than 65, with odds ratios of 1.74 (99% CI 0.94 to 3.21), 1.38 (1.09 to 1.76) and 1.29 (0.96 to 1.73) and no significant interaction (P = 0.313) ([Smyth 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehab738)). The myocardial infarction association held in all age groups ([Smyth 2016](https://doi.org/10.1161/circulationaha.116.023142)), frequent anger showed no interaction with age between 56 and 94 years ([Titova 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/ehjopen/oeac050)), and high anger in young men predicted cardiovascular disease before age 55 (relative risk 3.1, 95% CI 1.1 to 8.6) but not later disease ([Chang 2002](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinte.162.8.901)). Younger drivers are also more likely to report road rage as perpetrators and victims ([Smart 2003](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf03403543)). Because absolute cardiovascular risk rises steeply with age, similar relative risks imply that most attributable events fall among older commuters, while the largest proportional effects and the highest exposure prevalence sit in working-age adults.

## Contested points

Whether car time is harmful beyond what it displaces is unresolved. The single positive mortality cohort measured riding in a car once, in 1982, in men ([Warren 2010](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e3181c3aa7e)); two cohorts with driving or transport sitting as a separate domain were null ([Basterra-Gortari 2014](https://doi.org/10.1161/jaha.114.000864); [Kim 2013](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyt108)); and New York car commuters did not differ from transit commuters in inflammatory markers, possibly because they were more active overall ([Morabia 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ypmed.2012.01.019)). Whether sitting has any effect independent of physical activity is equally disputed: the harmonised analyses indicate that high activity removes the excess ([Ekelund 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0140-6736%2816%2930370-1); [Ekelund 2019a](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-098963)), while accelerometer data show a residual risk of heart failure and cardiovascular death among people who meet activity guidelines ([Ajufo 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2024.10.065)) and the Women's Health Initiative found no interaction between sitting and activity (P = 0.94) ([Chomistek 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2013.03.031)). The shape of the dose-response also differs between syntheses, from a threshold near 6 hours ([Patterson 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10654-018-0380-1)) to no excess below about 10 hours ([Pandey 2016](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamacardio.2016.1567)). For traffic, the roughly threefold odds of infarction after time in traffic ([Peters 2004](https://doi.org/10.1056/nejmoa040203)) cannot be apportioned among stress, particulates and noise, although filtration trials isolate a pollutant effect on blood pressure ([Young 2023](https://doi.org/10.7326/m23-1309)). For housing, the direction from costs to commute length is supported, but the magnitudes are small in the one study that quantified them directly ([Ahrens 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/0042098020910698)), and gentrification measures for the Bay Area disagree on which tracts are affected ([Mujahid 2019](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph16122246)).

## Gap this study fills

No retrieved study follows the chain from housing costs through commute duration to cardiovascular risk in any US region. PubMed returned 16 records combining housing costs, affordability or displacement with commuting, 11 combining the Bay Area or California with commuting and housing or health, and one on super-commuting; on title screening none had a cardiovascular outcome. The only California ecological precedent uses county-level data from 2001 and obesity as the endpoint ([Lopez-Zetina 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.healthplace.2005.09.001)), and the Bay Area commuting studies stop at distance ([Blumenberg 2024a](https://doi.org/10.5198/jtlu.2024.2456)). A tract-level analysis of cardiovascular prevalence against long-commute and drive-alone shares across the nine counties and the adjacent super-commuter counties would be new in geography, resolution and period.

No retrieved study estimates the cardiovascular burden attributable to commuting time. Burden estimates exist for sitting in general, for example a population-attributable fraction of 6.9% for all-cause mortality in Australia ([van der Ploeg 2012](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinternmed.2011.2174)) and 8.5% of first myocardial infarctions for anger or emotional upset ([Smyth 2016](https://doi.org/10.1161/circulationaha.116.023142)), but none allocates either to driving. An age-stratified comparative risk assessment for commuting would be the first, and its uncertainty analysis has to carry three declared transfers: from generic sitting to car sitting, from generic anger to anger while driving, and from all-age relative risks to age strata.

The age question is open in the source literature itself. Driving-specific estimates by age exist only as an unreported figure ([Warren 2010](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e3181c3aa7e)); age-stratified sitting estimates exist for leisure sitting and all-cause mortality ([Larsson 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2018.11.020)); and age-stratified anger estimates exist for stroke ([Smyth 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehab738)). Combining Bay Area age-specific commute distributions with these functions, and showing how conclusions change when relative risks are held constant across age or allowed to decline with age, addresses a question none of the 102 papers reviewed here answers.

## Parameters for the burden model

The extracted estimates are in the companion table [dose_response_parameters.csv]({{artifact:9e3d56d3-346c-482c-9498-04cf5dc18d64}}) (62 rows from 43 papers), and three choices follow from the evidence above. For the sitting pathway, the physical-activity-adjusted function of 1.02 per hour of daily sitting for cardiovascular mortality, rising to 1.04 per hour above 6 hours ([Patterson 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10654-018-0380-1)), is the most defensible central estimate, with 1.05 per hour for fatal and non-fatal events ([Onagbiye 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ypmed.2023.107812)) as an upper alternative and the car-specific categories of 1.09, 1.23 and 1.50 for 4 to 7, 7 to 10 and more than 10 hours a week ([Warren 2010](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e3181c3aa7e)) as a driving-specific sensitivity analysis. Because activity removes most of the excess, the model should apply the sitting function to the insufficiently active share of commuters or scale it by activity level ([Ekelund 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0140-6736%2816%2930370-1)). For the anger pathway, a rate ratio near 2.4 for myocardial infarction in the one to two hours after an episode ([Mostofsky 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amjcard.2013.03.035); [Smyth 2016](https://doi.org/10.1161/circulationaha.116.023142)) and 1.37 for stroke with the published age strata ([Smyth 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehab738)) are usable only with an assumed frequency of driving-related anger, for which the Ontario prevalence figures are the nearest population data ([Smart 2003](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf03403543)). For the blood-pressure pathway, the commuting-distance odds ratios for elevated blood pressure ([Hoehner 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amepre.2012.02.020)) are the only commute-specific estimates and come from a clinic sample.

## Scope and limits of this review

The sweep ran 38 PubMed queries (650 records), one citation-graph step in both directions from eleven anchor papers (2,425 additional records, keyword-filtered and then screened by title), and 16 CrossRef bibliographic queries for transport and planning journals that PubMed does not index. OpenAlex was not used. The full query list is in [search_log.csv]({{artifact:4eb2b271-4d4a-4ef4-902c-594c08bb0733}}) and the bibliography with reading depth per paper is in [references.csv]({{artifact:6c894647-e6c8-4430-b6a8-17bac4aa7e46}}). Of the 102 papers cited, 23 were read in full text, 70 from the abstract and 9 from title and bibliographic record only; the last group is cited solely for the existence and topic of the work. Thirty-one of the 62 parameter rows carry numbers read from an abstract or a press release: 25 because the full text was not retrievable (including the harmonised sitting meta-analyses, the INTERHEART trigger study and the traffic-trigger study), 5 where the full text was retrieved but the headline estimate was taken from the abstract, and 1 from a press release. The pooled anger-trigger estimate from the nine-study meta-analysis is not stated in its abstract and was obtained only from the journal's press release, so it is marked provisional in the table and is not used in the text above. Coverage of economics and planning journals is thinner than coverage of biomedical ones, and grey literature on super-commuting (census and regional-agency reports) was outside the search.
